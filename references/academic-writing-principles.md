# Academic Writing Principles

Reference for how admissions committees read application documents, common writing failures, and coaching approaches for voice, structure, and authenticity. Used by `review`, `draft`, and throughout document coaching.

---

## How Committees Read SoPs

Understanding the reading context is essential for coaching decisions.

### The Reading Environment

- A typical admissions committee reader reviews 50-300 applications per cycle
- Time per SoP in the first pass: 3-5 minutes
- The first 2 paragraphs determine whether the reader leans forward or leans back
- Most readers are looking for reasons to advance or cut — they are not neutral

### What Readers Notice First (in order)

1. **The opening line**: Does it signal a specific research domain and an interesting angle, or is it generic?
2. **The research credentials**: Any publications? Specific project names and outcomes?
3. **The program-fit paragraph**: Is this specific to this program and professor, or clearly a template?
4. **The voice**: Does this sound like a real person, or like a document that was over-edited?

### The "Pile Test"

A useful frame: "This document is one of 150 in a pile. What would make a reader remember it 3 days later?"

Most SoPs describe the same arc: interested in field → did research → want to study at your program → have big goals. The pile test asks what makes this one different from the other 149.

**Passing the pile test requires**:
- A distinctive intellectual angle (Distinctiveness ≥ 4)
- A specific research connection to this program's faculty (Fit ≥ 4)
- An intellectual insight that a committee member would want to discuss (Message ≥ 4)

## Show, Don't Tell

The most common failure in academic writing is assertion without evidence. In an SoP, this looks like:

| Telling (weak) | Showing (strong) |
|---------------|-----------------|
| "I am passionate about machine learning." | "After finding that [method X] failed on our imbalanced dataset, I spent three weeks redesigning the preprocessing pipeline — this was the problem I couldn't stop thinking about." |
| "I am a strong researcher." | "My contribution to the lab's [project] resulted in [specific outcome], which [specific significance]." |
| "I have strong analytical skills." | [Don't say this at all — demonstrate it through research descriptions that show analytical thinking] |
| "I am committed to interdisciplinary research." | "My undergraduate work in [A] changed how I think about [B], specifically by showing me that [intellectual insight connecting the two]." |

**The evidence hierarchy** (from strongest to weakest):
1. Quantified outcomes (publication count, accuracy improvement, sample size)
2. Named artifacts (conference poster, thesis chapter, submitted manuscript)
3. Specific role and contribution ("I designed the preprocessing pipeline, not just implemented it")
4. Methods and tools named specifically (not just "I did data analysis" but "I used wavelet transforms to analyze...")
5. Intellectual insight (the non-obvious thing learned)
6. Assertions without evidence (weakest — avoid)

## Paragraph-Level Structure

Strong academic writing has structural clarity at the paragraph level, not just the document level.

**Effective paragraph structure**:
```
Topic sentence: What this paragraph is about and what it claims
Evidence: Specific details that support the claim
Analysis: Why this evidence matters — what it shows or implies
Bridge: Connection to the next paragraph or to the document's main argument
```

**Common paragraph failures**:
- No topic sentence: paragraph starts mid-thought, reader doesn't know where it's going
- Evidence without analysis: "I worked on [project]. We found [result]." — so what?
- Analysis without evidence: "I am a capable researcher who brings unique perspectives." — show me
- No bridge: paragraph ends; next paragraph starts as if the previous didn't exist

## Voice and Authenticity

### What Authentic Voice Sounds Like

An authentic SoP sounds like an intelligent person talking about something they genuinely care about — with enough formal register to be appropriate for an academic application. It does NOT sound like:
- A template being filled in
- AI-generated text (uniform sentence structure, appropriate vocabulary used just slightly wrong)
- A consultant's polished rewrite of what the applicant said
- A formal academic essay

**Test**: "Does this sound like how [applicant's name] would talk about their research if you met them at a coffee hour?"

### Common Authenticity Destroyers

**Over-editing**:
- The applicant's original words had texture; editing removed the specific details and left only formal structure
- Fix: return to an earlier draft; ask the applicant to read it aloud — does it sound like them?

**Consultant voice**:
- The document sounds polished but has no personality
- Tell-tale signs: perfect grammar throughout with no natural variation; no specific details; everything stated at a level of abstraction that could apply to anyone
- Fix: ask the applicant to describe their research to you in their own words; transcribe the best parts

**AI-generated or heavily AI-edited text**:
- Signs: uniform sentence length, appropriate vocabulary used just slightly wrong, no natural cadence variation
- Fix: identify the sections that sound "off"; replace them with the applicant's own language, minimally refined

### Recovering Authentic Voice

When a document has lost its voice:
1. Ask the applicant: "Tell me about [the research project in P2] like you're explaining it to a smart friend, not an admissions committee."
2. Write down what they say (close paraphrase)
3. Compare to the document text
4. Use their spoken language as the basis for revision — edit for grammar and appropriateness, not for formality level

## Culturally Inflected Writing Advice

For international applicants or applicants from non-US academic traditions:

### Direct vs. Hedged Language

US academic applications use confident, direct assertions. Some academic traditions use hedged, collective, or formal language that reads as weakness in a US context.

| Non-US convention | US convention |
|------------------|---------------|
| "It might be said that..." | "I argue that..." |
| "The research was conducted..." | "I conducted..." |
| "We believe this finding shows..." | "This finding shows..." |
| "It is humbly submitted that..." | [Just state the claim] |
| "I had the opportunity to..." | "I [did the thing]" |

**Coaching note**: This is not about abandoning one's cultural voice — it's about adjusting register to the specific genre of the US academic application. The committee reads these conventions as signals of confidence and clarity, not arrogance.

### First Person and Individual Credit

Some academic cultures emphasize collective work and downplay individual contribution. In US PhD applications, committees want to understand what *you specifically* did.

If the applicant uses "we" throughout: gently ask them to claim individual credit where it's honest:
> "Were there parts of this project that were specifically your responsibility? Where you made decisions that others didn't? Let's make sure those are attributed to you."

### Length Norms

Some academic traditions write longer, more comprehensive applications. US SoPs have specific length conventions (typically 500-1000 words for PhD programs). Committees often penalize documents that significantly exceed the limit — it signals poor judgment about audience.

## What Coaching Covers vs. Doesn't Cover

**What this skill coaches**:
- Narrative structure and arc
- Voice, authenticity, and register
- Evidence and specificity of claims
- Program fit and differentiation
- Document conventions and genre expectations

**What this skill does NOT coach** (note the boundary explicitly):
- Whether the applicant's research findings are correct or significant (domain expertise required)
- Whether the proposed methods in a research proposal are sound (verify with advisor)
- Whether a writing sample's scholarly arguments are valid (discipline-specific judgment)
- Whether a program is a good fit based on factors outside the application materials (the applicant knows their field better than we do)

When encountering something that seems methodologically or factually questionable: "This might be worth reviewing with your advisor before submitting — I can coach the writing clarity but not evaluate the research correctness."
