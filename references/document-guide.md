# Document Guide

Reference for document types, structural templates, and common failure modes. Used by `draft-sop`, `draft`, `review`, and `prep`.

---

## Document Types Overview

| Document | When Required | Primary Evaluation | Typical Length |
|----------|--------------|-------------------|---------------|
| Statement of Purpose (SoP) | Most PhD programs; some Master's | Research fit, intellectual potential | 500–1000 words |
| Motivation Letter / Personal Statement | Fellowships (Fulbright, Gates); some programs | Personal narrative, mission alignment | 400–700 words |
| Research Proposal | Some PhD programs; NSF GRFP; some fellowships | Research quality, feasibility, significance | 500–2000 words |
| Writing Sample | Humanities/social science PhD | Academic writing quality, argumentation | 15–25 pages (usually existing work) |
| Diversity Statement | Some US PhD programs | Personal background, contribution to community | 250–500 words |
| Academic CV | All PhD programs | Research experience, publications, trajectory | 2–5 pages (no limit) |

---

## Statement of Purpose (SoP)

### What the committee is actually evaluating
- Do you have specific, relevant research experience?
- Do you have a clear research question you want to pursue?
- Do you know why this program specifically is the right place to pursue it?
- Are you a person they can picture succeeding in their program?

### Standard Architecture (research-focused PhD)

```
P1: Hook (2-3 sentences)
   → Specific research question or intellectual moment that opened the problem
   → NOT: "Ever since I was young..." / "I have always been passionate about..."

P2: Research Experience 1 (150-200 words)
   → Most relevant/recent project
   → Specific: what you did, what you found, your intellectual contribution
   → Include: one Intellectual Insight from this experience

P3: Research Experience 2 (100-150 words)
   → Complementary project (different method, domain, or perspective)
   → Can be shorter than P2

P4 (optional): Methodological bridge or additional experience
   → Use if you're a field switcher with a prior domain to explain
   → Or if three projects are each meaningful

P5: Your Research Agenda (100-150 words)
   → The specific question(s) you want to pursue in your PhD
   → Not generic goals: a specific, defensible intellectual agenda
   → What do you think the answer is, and why?

P6: Program Fit (100-150 words)
   → Prof. [Name]: specific recent work + how it connects to your work + what you'd contribute
   → Optional: second professor if there's genuine fit
   → One non-faculty element: a specific resource, seminar, or community
   → THIS PARAGRAPH IS WRITTEN FROM SCRATCH FOR EACH PROGRAM

P7: Goals (50-75 words)
   → Career direction after the PhD
   → Brief and specific — let the research speak
   → Do not end with: "I believe [Program] will help me achieve my goals"
```

### Common Failure Modes

**Credential Recitation (SoP reads like an annotated CV)**
- Symptom: "At University X, I worked in Lab Y. Then at Z, I did A and B."
- Fix: "While I worked in Lab Y, I encountered [problem]. I found [result]. This taught me [insight]." — narrative, not resume.

**Generic Program Fit (Fit = 1)**
- Symptom: "The program's resources and faculty are perfect for my interests."
- Fix: Name the specific professor, reference their specific recent work, connect it to your specific prior work. See `fit [program]`.

**Future Goals Bloat**
- Symptom: Long paragraph about career goals and plans with no specificity
- Fix: Goals should be 2-4 sentences maximum. Let the research experience carry the SoP.

**No Intellectual Insight**
- Symptom: SoP describes what happened but not what was learned
- Fix: End every research paragraph with the non-obvious takeaway from that experience

## Motivation Letter / Personal Statement

### What the committee is actually evaluating
For fellowships: mission alignment, personal motivation, evidence of genuine engagement, and (for some fellowships) character and leadership.

### Key difference from SoP
The MotLetter is more personal. It can open with a specific moment or experience in a way that would be unusual in an SoP. The register is less formal. The committee wants to understand *why* you care, not just *what* you've done.

### Standard Architecture (fellowship personal statement)

```
P1: Opening moment (1-2 sentences)
   → A specific experience, conversation, or observation that crystallized your motivation
   → More personal register than SoP

P2: Why this work matters to you (100-150 words)
   → The personal and intellectual stakes — why this research area / fellowship mission matters
   → Connect your background to the fellowship's stated values

P3: Your strongest qualification (100-150 words)
   → The most compelling evidence of your preparation
   → Usually a specific research experience with an intellectual insight

P4: Future goals and how the fellowship enables them (75-100 words)
   → Specific enough to be credible; connected to the fellowship's mission

P5: Closing (2-3 sentences)
   → What you will bring, not just what you will gain
   → Fellowship-specific: cultural exchange for Fulbright; intellectual contribution for Gates Cambridge; service for Truman
```

### Common Failure Modes

**Confusing MotLetter with SoP**
- Symptom: MotLetter reads like a shorter SoP — research-heavy, no personal register
- Fix: Increase authenticity register; add personal texture; connect to the fellowship's specific mission, not just academic credentials

**Generic Mission Alignment**
- Symptom: "Fulbright's mission of cultural exchange aligns with my values."
- Fix: Specific connection between the fellowship's mission and this applicant's specific plans: "My proposed project in [country] specifically requires [cultural collaboration X], which is why Fulbright's exchange mission is integral to the work itself, not just a nice-to-have."

## Research Proposal

### What the committee is actually evaluating
- Is this a viable research question?
- Does the applicant understand the relevant literature well enough to position this question?
- Is the proposed methodology plausible?
- Is this achievable in the proposed timeline?
- Is the applicant the right person to pursue this?

### Standard Architecture (NSF GRFP-style research proposal)

```
Title (descriptive, not creative)

Problem Statement / Motivation (100-200 words)
   → What is the problem?
   → Why does it matter? (broader significance)
   → What's missing from current approaches?

Research Objectives (50-100 words)
   → 2-3 specific, achievable objectives
   → Bullet points or numbered list acceptable

Methods (200-400 words)
   → How will you address each objective?
   → Be specific about data, tools, analysis approaches
   → Acknowledge limitations and how you'll handle them

Expected Outcomes and Significance (100-200 words)
   → What do you expect to find?
   → What would it mean if you're right?
   → What would it mean if you're wrong (null result scenario)?

Timeline (brief — often a table)
   → Reasonable milestones; shows you've thought about execution

Broader Impacts (if NSF GRFP) — NOT an afterthought
   → Educational, social, or other non-academic contributions of this research
   → Must be substantive and specific; weighted equally with Intellectual Merit
```

### Note on coaching scope
"I'm coaching the clarity and structure of your proposal, not evaluating the correctness of your research methodology. If something seems methodologically off, I'll flag it, but you should verify with your advisor."

## Diversity Statement (where required)

### What the committee is actually evaluating
This varies significantly by program, but typically: evidence that the applicant's background or experience will contribute to the intellectual and cultural diversity of the program.

### What it is NOT
- Not a hardship narrative (though difficulty can be mentioned if relevant)
- Not a demographic box-checking exercise
- Not a place to list your accomplishments (that's the SoP)

### Approach
Focus on what you bring to the program's community that would otherwise be absent. This can be cultural background, unusual intellectual perspective, experience with underrepresented communities in research, non-traditional path, or other factors.

## Writing Sample

Typically required for humanities and social science PhD programs.

### Selection criteria
1. Most relevant to the target program's methodology and style
2. Independently written (not collaborative work)
3. Most recent (shows current capabilities, not past capabilities)
4. Already polished — don't submit a draft

### Common mistakes
- Selecting a seminar paper that hasn't been revised for a wider audience
- Selecting work that uses a methodology not common in the target program
- Not checking length requirements (most programs specify 15-25 pages)

Coaching note: "I can review the structure and argumentation of your writing sample, but the specific content and scholarly judgments are yours."
