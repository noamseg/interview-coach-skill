# lor — Letter of Recommendation Coaching

**Purpose**: Coach the applicant through the LoR process — selecting recommenders, making effective requests, and providing recommenders with the materials they need to write specific, compelling letters. LoRs are third-party documents the applicant doesn't write, but they profoundly influence their quality.

## 1) select — Identify and Rank Recommenders

Most PhD programs require 3 letters. Some require 2; some accept up to 5. Confirm the number required before selecting.

**Evaluation criteria for recommenders**:

Ask the applicant to list all potential recommenders. For each, assess:

| Criterion | Questions to Ask |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Knowledge depth** | How well do they know your work? Can they describe specific projects, contributions, and intellectual qualities? |
| **Relationship recency** | How recent is the relationship? A letter from someone who knew you 5 years ago is weaker than one from last year, unless they've maintained contact. |
| **Enthusiasm signal** | Have they expressed enthusiasm for your work or your application? Or are they agreeing to write out of obligation? |
| **Letter quality history** | Do you know if they've written strong letters for others? Former students can sometimes tell you. |
| **Institutional weight** | A letter from a well-known researcher in your field carries more weight than one from someone the committee won't recognize — but only if the content is strong. |
| **Role diversity** | Ideally: a mix of recommenders who can speak to different aspects (research mentor + academic instructor + relevant professional) |

**Ranking protocol**:

> "Let's rank your potential recommenders. I'll ask you about each one, and we'll figure out your best 3."

For each candidate: ask about knowledge depth, recency, and enthusiasm signal. Then rank.

**Flag situations**:
- "You have 3 recommenders but 2 of them are from the same lab" → consider whether this gives the committee a one-dimensional view
- "Your strongest recommender is someone you haven't worked with in 3 years" → ask if you've maintained contact; if not, send a re-engagement email before the request
- "None of your recommenders are research mentors" → for a research-focused PhD, this is a significant weakness. Committees expect at least 1-2 letters from research supervisors.
- "You're planning to ask someone who said they'd write but didn't seem enthusiastic" → see the strong letter test below

**The strong letter test**:

> "Before you ask each recommender, you should ask them directly: 'Do you feel you know my work well enough to write me a strong letter?' Give them a genuine out. Someone who says 'yes, absolutely' will write a different letter than someone who says 'I'll do my best.' You want only 'absolutely' recommenders."

## 2) request — Draft the Request Email

**Timing**: Request letters 6-8 weeks before the deadline. Less than 4 weeks is an emergency; some recommenders will decline or write rushed letters. The applicant should also provide deadline reminders 2 weeks and 1 week before.

**Draft the email for each recommender**. Personalize each one — the email to a research advisor is different from the email to a course professor.

**Elements of a strong request email**:

1. **The ask**: Clear, direct, early in the email. "I'm writing to ask if you would be willing to write a letter of recommendation for my PhD applications."

2. **Why you chose them**: "I'm asking you specifically because [specific shared experience and what you learned from it]." This is not flattery — it's reminding the recommender what they can speak to.

3. **The strong letter check**: "If you feel you know my work well enough to write a strong letter, I'd be very grateful. If you're too busy or feel you can't write a detailed letter, please don't hesitate to say so — I completely understand."

4. **Context**: "I'm applying to PhD programs in [field], focusing on [programs]. My primary research interest is [brief 1-2 sentences]."

5. **Package offer**: "I'll put together a recommender brief with my CV, a draft of my SoP, the list of programs and deadlines, and a summary of the work we did together — to make writing as easy as possible for you."

6. **Deadline info**: "The earliest deadline is [date]. I'll provide all materials well in advance."

**Template structure**:

```
Subject: Letter of Recommendation Request — [Your Name] — PhD Applications

Dear Prof. [Name],

I hope you're doing well. I'm writing to ask if you would be willing to write a letter of recommendation for my PhD applications.

I'm asking you specifically because [specific shared experience — the project/course/collaboration and what they observed about your work]. I believe you're well-positioned to speak to [what you want them to highlight: your research capability / your intellectual curiosity / your ability to work independently / etc.].

If you feel you know my work well enough to write a strong letter, I'd be very grateful. If you're too busy or feel you can't write a detailed letter at this time, please don't hesitate to say so — I completely understand.

I'm applying to PhD programs in [field], with a focus on [your research area]. My top programs include [2-3 programs]. The earliest application deadline is [date].

I'll put together a complete recommender brief — including my CV, a draft personal statement, program list and deadlines, and a summary of the work we did together — to make this as easy as possible for you.

Would you be willing to write? I'd be happy to chat if you'd like to hear more about my plans.

Thank you for considering this.

[Your name]
```

## 3) brief — Create the Recommender Brief

The recommender brief is the highest-leverage thing the applicant can do to improve their LoR quality. It gives the recommender the material to write a specific, differentiated letter without having to remember everything from years ago.

**Brief contents for each recommender**:

**Section 1: Your Materials**
- Academic CV (current)
- Draft personal statement (or SoP draft)
- List of all programs with deadlines (formatted for easy reference)
- Link to each program's application portal (where they'll submit)

**Section 2: Our Shared Work** (personalized per recommender)
This is the most important section. Remind the recommender of:
- The specific project(s) you worked on together
- Your specific contributions — what you did, not just that you were there
- Any moments they may remember: a discussion, a result you found, a problem you solved
- What you contributed that was distinctive (your intellectual insight, your independence, your creativity)

*Example*: "In Dr. Smith's lab (Spring 2023), I designed the preprocessing pipeline for the EEG study on attention decoding. The specific challenge was handling the artifact contamination in trials with eye movements — I came up with the rejection threshold approach that became the lab's standard protocol. We discussed this problem in our weekly meeting in March; I remember you said the solution was more elegant than you'd expected."

**Section 3: What I'm Hoping You Can Speak To**
Without putting words in their mouth, tell the recommender what aspects of your work you're hoping they can highlight:

*Example*: "If it's possible, I'd especially appreciate if you could speak to: my ability to work independently once I understood the problem, my methodological creativity (the preprocessing solution above), and my engagement with the broader intellectual questions behind the project — I often came to our meetings with questions about why the patterns we were finding had the interpretations they did, not just whether the code was running."

**Section 4: My Research Plans**
2-3 sentences on what you're planning to study in your PhD and why. This helps the recommender frame your past work as preparation for future research.

**Section 5: Brag Sheet**
The moments you're most proud of in your work with this person — framed as material they can draw on, not as boasting:

*Example*: "Some moments from our work together that you might want to reference: The conference poster I presented at [venue] based on our lab's data — I built the entire analysis pipeline independently. The time I caught the calibration error that had been corrupting 3 months of data — and the way I worked through it systematically to figure out which sessions could be salvaged."

```markdown

|-------------|-------------|----------|----------|--------|
| [Name] | Research Advisor | All | [date] | Requested |

| [Name] | Industry Supervisor | [1 program] | [date] | Not yet requested |

[Full brief content from the sections above]
```

```
Warning: [Program]'s deadline is [date]. [Recommender]'s letter has not been submitted.
Action needed: Email [Recommender] today with a polite reminder.
```

**Follow-up cadence**:
- 2 weeks before deadline: send a reminder with the application portal link
- 1 week before deadline: confirm receipt in the portal; if not yet submitted, send an urgent follow-up
- Day of deadline: check the portal; if still missing, consider whether to contact the program to ask for a brief extension

## 5) waive — Waiving the Right to Review

Most programs ask whether the applicant waives their right to review the letter.

**General guidance**: Waive it.

Why: Recommenders know that applicants who waive their right have letters that are considered more credible by committees. A letter with the waiver unchecked signals to committees that the applicant may have read and potentially influenced the letter. If the recommender isn't trusted enough to waive, they shouldn't be a recommender.

**Exception**: If the applicant has serious reason to doubt a recommender (prior bad relationship, rumor of weak letters), they should not ask that person at all rather than not waiving.

**Script if the applicant is nervous about waiving**:
> "If you're worried about what [recommender] might write, that's a signal to ask someone else. The goal is recommenders you trust enough to waive. If you trust them enough to ask, trust them enough to waive."
