# Cross-Cutting Modules

These modules are active across all workflows. Referenced from SKILL.md and integrated into specific commands as noted.

---

## Module 1: Differentiation Module

**Always active.** Distinctiveness is the 5th scoring dimension and is checked in every `review` session.

**Core principle**: Every document should pass the Substitution Test — if you replace the applicant's name with another qualified applicant in the same field, does the document still work? If yes, Distinctiveness = 1-2.

**Integration points**:
- `review`: Scored as Distinctiveness dimension; auto-rewrite of weakest section targets the most generic section
- `draft [sharpen]`: Dedicated drill for making claims more distinctive
- `narratives add/improve`: Intellectual Insight extraction is the primary distinctiveness mechanism
- `positioning`: Spiky POV development creates the core distinctive angle
- `progress`: Distinctiveness trend tracked alongside other dimensions

**Quick diagnostic**: "What would a committee member remember about this application 1 week after reading it?" If the answer is "nothing specific," Distinctiveness is the bottleneck.

See `references/differentiation.md` for full intellectual insight extraction protocol.

## Module 2: Narrative Gap-Handling Module

**Active in**: `narratives`, `draft`, `review`, `prep`

When an applicant doesn't have a strong narrative for a required document section, there are 4 patterns to choose from:

### Pattern 1: Adjacent Bridge
Use a narrative from a related but different domain, with explicit framing that acknowledges the gap.

*Example*: No lab research experience, but has industry research. "While I haven't published in academic venues, my work at [company] on [project] required the same core skills: forming a hypothesis, designing a test, collecting data, and drawing conclusions under uncertainty. The difference was timeline and accountability, not methodology."

When to use: when the adjacent experience is genuinely relevant and can be honestly framed as building the same skill.

### Pattern 2: Hypothetical With Self-Awareness
Acknowledge the gap honestly, then articulate what the applicant brings instead and why that's sufficient.

*Example*: No research publications. "I don't have publications from my undergraduate work. What I do have is [X sessions of guided research / thesis work / directed study] that gave me [specific skills]. I'm applying to programs with strong mentorship precisely because I want to develop the independent research skills that publication requires."

When to use: when the gap is real and can't be bridged; honesty + forward-looking framing is better than forced reframing.

### Pattern 3: Reframe to Strength
What looks like a gap from one angle is a strength from another.

*Example*: International student with unusual academic path. "My training in [country's academic system] differs significantly from US graduate norms, but it gave me [specific advantage: e.g., 'early exposure to real experimental work, since coursework and lab work were integrated rather than sequential']."

When to use: when the "gap" is actually a genuine difference that can be honestly positioned as additive.

### Pattern 4: Development Narrative
The applicant is aware of the gap and is actively working to close it.

*Example*: No second research project. "My research experience has been focused on one project, which I chose deliberately to go deep rather than wide. I am aware that breadth matters for PhD programs, which is one reason I'm targeting programs where [X approach to breadth is available]."

When to use: when the applicant is genuinely developing and can articulate the plan.

## Module 3: Feedback Signal-Reading Module

**Active in**: `postsubmit`, `feedback`, `progress`

After submitting applications and receiving responses (from programs, from professors), the applicant needs help reading weak signals.

### Common signals and interpretations

| Signal | Interpretation | Confidence |
|--------|---------------|------------|
| Professor responds enthusiastically to cold email | Positive fit signal; apply with confidence | MEDIUM |
| Professor doesn't respond to cold email | No signal either way; apply normally | HIGH (absence ≠ rejection) |
| Professor responds with "I'm not taking students" | Clear signal: apply to other faculty at this program, or reconsider | HIGH |
| Program sends automated acknowledgment | No signal | HIGH |
| Program requests interview | Strong positive signal: past document review stage | HIGH |
| Program goes quiet after interview | No signal until decision | HIGH |
| Waitlisted | Mixed signal: competitive application but others ranked higher; outcome can change | MEDIUM |
| Rejected without feedback | No specific signal about what went wrong | HIGH |
| Rejected with brief feedback | Treat this as high-value data; capture verbatim | HIGH |

### Post-decision signal capture

After receiving a decision, ask:
1. "Did you receive any feedback from the program?"
2. "Did any professor communicate with you before or after the decision?"
3. "Were there any patterns across accepted/rejected programs (e.g., fit strength, document scores)?"

Compare against score data. If a high-scoring document resulted in rejection: investigate potential non-document factors (credential gap, competitive pool, no faculty match). If a lower-scoring document resulted in admission: update calibration.

## Module 4: Program Fit Assessment Module

**Active in**: `research`, `prep`, `fit`, `decode`

The 5-dimension fit assessment used across research and prep workflows:

1. **Research Fit**: Is there specific overlap between the applicant's prior work and the professor's current projects?
2. **Academic Alignment**: Do the applicant's credentials match the program's typical admit profile?
3. **Domain Relevance**: Is the field/subfield itself a match?
4. **Professor Match**: Are there 2-3 specific faculty viable as advisors? (Single match = risky)
5. **Trajectory Coherence**: Does the applicant's academic path make sense as preparation for this program?

**Verdict levels**:
- **Strong**: Well-positioned across all 5 dimensions
- **Investable Stretch**: 3-4 strong, 1-2 addressable gaps
- **Long-Shot Stretch**: Significant gaps in 2+ dimensions; exceptional documents needed
- **Weak**: Fundamental misalignment; don't apply

## Module 5: Psychological Readiness Module

**Active in**: `premit`, `kickoff` (anxiety profile), `coaching notes`, `reflect`

Academic application processes carry specific psychological burdens. Calibrate coaching tone based on Profile → Anxiety profile.

### Impostor Syndrome (most common among PhD applicants)

Signal: "I'm not really qualified / Everyone else is more impressive / My GPA/publications aren't good enough."

Coaching response:
- Lead with evidence, not encouragement. "Your score on [dimension] is [X]. Your advisor said [specific thing]. This isn't subjective — these are measured outcomes."
- Avoid: "You're great!" (feels patronizing and unconvincing to someone with impostor syndrome)
- Do: "Here's what the data shows. Here's what the committee will see." Concrete and specific.
- Flag in Coaching Notes for ongoing calibration.

### Reapplicant Trauma

Signal: History of rejection from previous application cycle. May present as "I'm not good enough" or excessive defensiveness about their application choices.

Coaching response:
- Acknowledge the previous rejection explicitly, early. "You've been through this before. That experience is real, and it's okay if it's still raw."
- Then: "The situation is different now. Here's what's changed: [specific improvements — new research, stronger narratives, better fit, clearer direction]."
- Do NOT: pretend the previous rejection didn't happen; frame everything as if it was a clean slate.

### Deadline Anxiety

Signal: Excessive focus on logistics, checklist-checking, spiraling about small details.

Coaching response:
- Narrow the aperture. "The only thing that matters right now is [the single most important unfinished task]. Everything else can wait."
- Give a concrete process to follow. Anxiety decreases with clear next steps.
- Avoid open-ended questions ("What do you want to work on?") — this can spiral. Give a prescription.

### Post-Rejection Processing

Signal: Received a rejection and needs to process before being ready to work.

Coaching response:
- Don't immediately pivot to "what can we learn." Allow space first.
- Then, when the applicant is ready: "Do you want to look at what this means for your other applications, or do you need more time first?"
- Only when the applicant signals readiness: feedback capture and strategic implications.

## Module 6: Cultural and Linguistic Awareness

**Active in**: `review`, `draft`, `academic-cv`, `kickoff`

For international applicants or applicants from non-US academic contexts:

### Writing Register
US academic writing values directness, confident assertions, and first-person voice ("I argue," "I found," "I will contribute"). Some academic traditions value more hedged, collective, or formal language.

Flag when review reveals: excessive hedging ("it might be argued that"), avoidance of first-person ("the research showed" where "I found" would be more direct), or formulaic phrases that don't sound natural in English.

Coaching approach: "In US academic applications, direct first-person assertions are expected and read as confidence, not arrogance. Let's adjust these phrases to sound more direct."

### Application Conventions
Some academic systems don't use SoPs in the US format. If an applicant is unfamiliar with the SoP genre, explain:
- The SoP is not a formal academic essay — it's a personal research narrative
- It should sound like an intelligent person talking about their work, not like a formal report
- The goal is to make the committee want to meet you, not impress them with complexity

### LoR norms
In some academic cultures, it's unusual to ask an advisor for a letter (it may feel presumptuous). Coaching response: "In US PhD applications, asking for LoRs is completely standard and expected. Advisors anticipate these requests. The ask is not unusual."
