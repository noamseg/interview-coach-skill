# Program Archetypes

Reference for program culture types and fellowship-specific review criteria. Used by `research`, `prep`, and `decode`.

---

## PhD Program Archetypes

### Archetype 1: R1 Research-First PhD

**Examples**: MIT, Stanford, Berkeley, Harvard, Caltech, Carnegie Mellon (top-ranked research universities)

**What they value most**:
- Research experience: publications, thesis chapters, or clear evidence of independent research capability
- Research fit: specific faculty match is often required (single-PI or advisor-based admission)
- Intellectual potential: evidence that you can generate and pursue original research questions

**SoP implications**:
- Lead with research, not goals
- Program-fit paragraph is critical — generic fit paragraphs are immediately visible in a competitive pool
- Publications or strong thesis work: prominently featured
- Goals section: brief; let research speak

**Admission signal norms**:
- Strong Admit requires: specific professor match + research experience with intellectual insight + above-median credentials
- Borderline: good credentials, weak professor fit, or vague research agenda

**What to NOT emphasize**:
- Coursework plans (committees care about research, not what classes you want to take)
- Career goals over research questions
- Long preambles before the research narrative

---

### Archetype 2: Coursework-Heavy Master's

**Examples**: Professional Master's programs, MEng programs, many terminal Master's

**What they value most**:
- Professional goals: clear career direction
- Academic preparation: relevant coursework and skills
- Program fit: how the curriculum serves the stated goals

**SoP implications**:
- Goals section is more prominent than in research PhDs
- Coursework relevance matters more
- Research experience is valuable but not always required
- Shorter, more professionally-toned SoPs often appropriate

**What to NOT do**:
- Frame as "I want to do research" if it's a professional Master's — that's a mismatch
- Overthink professor fit — many professional Master's don't have advisor-based admission

---

### Archetype 3: Interdisciplinary PhD Programs

**Examples**: Computational biology programs, environmental policy programs, data science programs, cognitive science programs

**What they value most**:
- Bridge narrative: the applicant's ability to work across two or more disciplines
- Cross-domain perspective as an asset, not a gap
- Research questions that genuinely require interdisciplinary approaches

**SoP implications**:
- The applicant's cross-domain background should be framed explicitly as the distinctive angle
- "My background in [A] and training in [B] allow me to approach [problem] in a way that pure [A] or [B] researchers cannot" — this is the distinctiveness claim
- Mention faculty from multiple departments if the program has cross-departmental advising

**Common mistake**: Applicant has an interdisciplinary background but writes a single-discipline SoP out of anxiety about seeming unfocused. Fix: the interdisciplinary framing is the strongest positioning for these programs.

### Archetype 4: Fellowship Programs

Each fellowship has specific review criteria. Decode the rubric before writing any document. See individual sections below.

---

## Fellowship Program Criteria

### NSF Graduate Research Fellowship Program (GRFP)

**Two explicitly weighted criteria** (weighted equally):
1. **Intellectual Merit**: potential to advance knowledge; prior research experience; academic excellence
2. **Broader Impacts**: potential to benefit society; educational activities; broadening participation in STEM

**Key insight**: Broader Impacts is NOT an afterthought. Reviewers are explicitly instructed to weight it equally with Intellectual Merit. Many otherwise strong proposals fail because the Broader Impacts section is 2 generic sentences at the end.

**What Broader Impacts means** (non-exhaustive):
- Educational outreach: teaching, mentoring, science communication
- Broadening participation: supporting underrepresented groups in STEM
- Societal benefit: direct application of the research to real-world problems
- Open science: making methods, data, or tools available to the community

**SoP implications**:
- Personal Statement: lead with personal journey and how you arrived at research + evidence of potential
- Research Proposal: structured around both Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts; both must be specific and substantive
- The "Intellectual Merit" and "Broader Impacts" headers are sometimes explicitly required in the proposal

**Common mistake**: Strong research proposal with generic Broader Impacts ("This research will benefit society by advancing scientific knowledge"). Fix: "I will mentor [number] underrepresented undergraduates through [specific program] at [institution], exposing them to [specific research experience]. Additionally, I will present this work at [specific public venue]."

### Fulbright U.S. Student Program

**What they evaluate**:
1. Project Statement: feasibility, clarity, relevance to your background
2. Personal Statement: your suitability as a cultural ambassador
3. Letters of Recommendation: corroboration of your personal and academic strengths
4. Country selection: does the project require being in this specific country?

**Key insight**: Fulbright is explicitly about cultural exchange. The program is administered by the State Department. They want ambassadors, not just scholars. The project must require being in the host country — not just be possible there.

**Project Statement implications**:
- "Why this country?" is one of the most important questions; the answer must be substantive and specific
- "Why now?" — how does this project fit your career stage and goals?
- Feasibility: do you have host institution affiliation? Language skills? Connections?

**Personal Statement implications**:
- Demonstrates that you're the kind of person who will represent the US well and engage genuinely with the host culture
- Cross-cultural experience (if any) is very relevant here
- Community engagement and service history matter

**Common mistake**: Treating Fulbright like an academic fellowship and writing a research-heavy proposal with minimal attention to cultural exchange. Fix: every section should contain explicit connection to the cultural exchange mission.

### Rhodes Scholarship

**Four criteria** (explicitly stated):
1. Literary and scholastic attainments
2. Fondness for and success in sports (broadly interpreted as physical vigor and leadership through activity)
3. Truth, courage, devotion to duty, sympathy for and protection of the weak, kindliness, unselfishness, and fellowship
4. Exhibition of moral force of character and instincts to lead, and to take an interest in one's fellow beings

**Key insight**: Rhodes is not primarily an academic scholarship. It is a character scholarship. Academic excellence is required but not sufficient. The "sports" criterion is interpreted broadly as evidence of leadership, teamwork, and engagement beyond academic life.

**Application implications**:
- Personal statement should demonstrate character as much as academic achievement
- Essay topics typically require discussion of challenges, values, and experiences with others
- Three letters of recommendation; at least one should know the applicant as a person, not just as a scholar
- The interview is often about current events, values, and intellectual engagement — not research specifics

**Common mistake**: Writing a Rhodes application like an academic fellowship application, leading with research achievements and neglecting character and engagement dimensions.

---

### Marshall Scholarship

**What they evaluate**:
- Academic achievement at a high level
- Leadership qualities and potential
- Commitment to understanding the relationship between the UK and US

**Key insight**: Marshall scholars must study at a UK institution. The program selects people who will genuinely engage with British academic and cultural life, and who will maintain that connection afterward.

**Application implications**:
- Clear rationale for why UK specifically (not just "UK has good universities")
- Evidence of leadership: examples of initiating, organizing, or driving something
- Academic excellence: typically GPA 3.8+ at a highly selective institution

---

### Gates Cambridge Scholarship

**Four criteria**:
1. Outstanding intellectual ability
2. Reasons for choice of course (why Cambridge specifically; why this research)
3. Commitment to improving the lives of others
4. Leadership capacity

**Key insight**: Gates Cambridge is explicitly about using intellectual ability in service of others. The "commitment to improving lives" criterion is not a minor add-on — it's core.

**Application implications**:
- The choice of Cambridge must be defended with specificity: specific supervisor, specific facilities, specific intellectual environment not available elsewhere
- Commitment to improving lives should be demonstrated through concrete actions, not stated values
- Personal Statement: opportunity to show depth of person, not just depth of research

## Using Program Archetypes in Coaching

When `research [program]` or `prep [program]` identifies a program archetype, use it to:
1. Calibrate which dimensions to emphasize in document coaching
2. Adjust the SoP architecture recommendation
3. Identify fellowship-specific section requirements
4. Set appropriate expectations for what a Strong Admit document looks like

When `decode [fellowship criteria]` is run on a fellowship prompt, cross-reference these archetype notes to validate the 6-lens analysis.
